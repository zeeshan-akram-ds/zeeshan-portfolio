Case studies / Discount profitability and margin erosion

Discounted sales earned $97 less profit per order line

Retail Discount Profitability Audit. Synthetic dataset (Microsoft Contoso): about 223,000 order lines across about 93,000 orders. PostgreSQL, Python and Power BI.

By Zeeshan Akram, founder of MarginLens.

The question

Discounting is one of the most common pricing levers, and one of the least measured. This audit asks whether discounting is a tactical lever or has become the business's pricing floor, and whether deeper discounts compress margin.

The numbers

  • Order lines sold below list priceObserved in the data61%
  • Profit per discounted line, against full-priceObserved in the data−$97
  • Margin gap between discounted and full-price linesObserved in the data4 pts

Observed figures are measured directly in the dataset. Modeled figures are what-ifs built from it, not savings or forecasts.

Findings

  • Margin fell from about 54% at low discount levels to about 49% in the 10% to 20% tier. The correlation between discount depth and margin was −0.37: moderate, and consistently negative. No tier showed margin recovering.
  • Computers and Cell Phones made up about 46% of high-discount volume and the largest total margin loss. That is a scale effect, not unusually deep discounts: margin compression was similar in every category, between about 4.9 and 5.3 points.
  • Discount rates and margins were nearly identical across stores, which points to central pricing policy rather than store-level discretion. The pattern was stable year over year, with no material deterioration.
  • 24.2% of customers bought at 90% or more discount dependency, and only 11% bought near full price.
  • Average margin fell with customer discount dependency: 53.5% for low, 51.7% for medium and 50.4% for high. Medium-dependency customers placed the most order lines per customer (6.23, against 3.20 for high), so more discounting did not buy more activity beyond a point.

What the audit concluded

RecommendationReview pricing policy before setting any more volume-led growth targets. In this data, discounting behaves like the default price, not a promotion.

Methodology

  • Grain: one row per product line within an order, from seven related tables (order rows, orders, customers, products, stores, dates and currency rates).
  • Margin: calculated per line as net price minus unit cost, divided by net price.
  • Sequence: eight SQL files, each answering one business question: baseline, discounted against full-price, margin by discount tier, category drivers, customer dependency, trend over time and store behavior.
  • Validation: Python checks and customer segmentation, then a three-page Power BI dashboard built around one question: is discounting tactical, or the pricing floor?
  • Tiers: discount bands at 5%, 10% and 20% were set to detect patterns. They do not come from a real pricing policy.

Limitations

  • The dataset is synthetic. It behaves like a business but is not one, so findings describe the data as structured and should not be read as real-world conclusions.
  • There is no elasticity data. The analysis cannot tell whether discounting created extra volume or only cut the price of sales that would have happened anyway.
  • Margin is line-level only. Fixed costs, returns and overheads are excluded.
  • Currency conversion was not applied. The focus is on relative measures such as margin and discount rate, which limits the effect but does not remove it for absolute comparisons across regions.
  • The correlation shows a relationship, not a cause. Every figure on this page is observed in the data. No modeled figures are used.
